4.1 · Characteristics of academic writing

Choose words that match the evidence.

Formal tone

Use a measured claim. Replace “rehearsal was really helpful” with a specific account of what was reported and by whom.

See the revision

Nine of twelve volunteers reported greater confidence after rehearsing.

Third-person perspective

Foreground the subject or evidence in a conventional essay. In a reflection, use first person when it identifies your experience or decision.

Compare two contexts

Essay: The table records a difference of one session. Reflection: I revised my claim after noticing that the sources measured different outcomes.

Diction

Choose precise verbs. Reported fits self-report, observed fits recorded behaviour and caused calls for evidence that can support causation.

See how the verb changes the claim

“Volunteers reported greater confidence” describes the available evidence. “Rehearsal increased confidence” makes a causal claim that the survey cannot establish.

4.1 · Academic conventions

Make a source easy to trace.

A citation identifies whose idea you used. A reference gives the reader enough information to retrieve the work. Track both while drafting.

Narrative and parenthetical citations

Narrative: List and Alexander (2019) propose a framework for using multiple texts.

Parenthetical: Multiple-text use can be understood as a process with distinct phases (List & Alexander, 2019).

When quoting a short passage, use quotation marks and a page or other suitable locator. When paraphrasing, cite the author and year.

Match the reference to the citation

List, A., & Alexander, P. A. (2019). Toward an integrated framework of multiple text use. Educational Psychologist, 54(1), 20–39. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2018.1505514

Check author order, year, title and source against the publication record. Use APA’s reference examples to check the format for the source type.

4.2 · Process of academic writing

Plan, draft and revise.

The stages give you a route through the work. Return to an earlier stage when a source changes the argument or the draft reveals a gap.

Pre-writing

Read the task carefully. Identify your audience and purpose, then use background reading to narrow the question. Record source details before you begin drafting.

While writing

Give each paragraph a purpose. Paraphrase accurately, support the claim with details, attribute them and explain what they show. Update the references as you write.

Post-writing

Revise the argument and support. Edit for clarity, tone and coherence. Proofread grammar, spelling and citation details after the argument is stable.

Claim. Peer rehearsal may help trainees feel prepared, although evidence of clearer classroom transitions remains limited.

Support. In Source A, nine of twelve volunteers reported greater confidence. Source B recorded clear transitions in six of twelve rehearsed sessions, compared with five of twelve unrehearsed sessions.

Reasoning and implication. The first result concerns perceived confidence and the second concerns observed behaviour. These results give little basis for predicting an individual trainee’s performance. A teacher educator could offer rehearsal as a preparation option and collect more comparable observation data before making it a programme requirement.
