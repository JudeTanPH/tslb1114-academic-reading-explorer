4.1 · Characteristics of academic writing

Choose words that match the evidence.

A teacher educator reading an essay about microteaching needs to know what happened, who was involved and how much confidence to place in the conclusion. Tone, perspective and diction help you make those decisions visible to the reader.

4.1.1 Formal tone

Use a measured register. Replace casual intensifiers, sweeping generalisations and vague approval with a specific claim. Set the strength of the claim by examining the evidence. A small survey can describe what its respondents reported. A stronger design is needed to establish what caused a change.

First attempt “Rehearsal was really helpful and everybody got better at teaching.”

Academic version “Nine of the twelve volunteers reported greater confidence in giving directions after rehearsing.”

What changed The sentence identifies who responded and what they reported. It leaves open whether their observed teaching improved. The example uses the simulated survey in the Writing Studio.

Qualify the part that is uncertain. In “The findings suggest that rehearsal may help some trainees feel prepared”, the uncertainty concerns the wider interpretation. A count directly stated in a source can usually be reported without hedging the count itself.

Hyland (2005) calls choices such as may and suggests part of a writer’s stance. Hedges leave room for uncertainty. Boosters such as clearly signal stronger commitment and need a defensible basis. Reader engagement concerns how a writer guides the reader, for example by directing attention to a meaningful difference in a table.

4.1.2 Third-person perspective

In a conventional evidence-based essay, put the concept, evidence or participants in the foreground. This helps readers see the basis for the claim. An active sentence such as “The observation table records a one-session difference” makes the source and action clear. A passive sentence can be useful when the procedure matters more than the person carrying it out, as in “Each session was rated using the same criterion”.

Academic essay “The observation table shows that six rehearsed sessions and five unrehearsed sessions met the transition criterion.”

Practicum reflection “I shortened my instructions after noticing that pupils began the activity at different times.”

Why the perspective changes The essay asks the reader to examine external evidence. The reflection asks the trainee to account for a professional decision. Use the perspective that suits the genre and assignment.

4.1.3 Diction

Choose words with the meaning your argument needs. “The group had good results” leaves the outcome and the basis for the judgement unclear. “The group completed more tasks within the allotted time” identifies the result that a reader can examine. Specify the comparison, the measure or the condition when it affects interpretation.

Reported
Use for what respondents or an author said. “Volunteers reported greater confidence.”
Observed
Use for a recorded behaviour. “Observers recorded a clear transition in six rehearsed sessions.”
Suggests
Use for a plausible interpretation within the limits of the evidence. “The comparison suggests a possible benefit worth investigating.”
Caused
Use when the evidence supports a causal link. Before writing “rehearsal caused the difference”, examine how the groups were formed and whether they were comparable.
Try a sentence revision

Revise “The teaching technique produced amazing results” using this detail: 8 of 10 pupils completed the activity within five minutes. A precise version is “Eight of the ten pupils completed the activity within five minutes.” A comparison with another technique would require comparable data.

4.1.4 · Academic conventions

Citations and APA 7 referencing

A citation tells the reader whose idea, data or wording you used. A reference gives the publication details needed to retrieve it. Together, they let a reader check the evidence behind your argument.

Decide what to cite and where

Cite borrowed ideas, data, interpretations and wording even when you change the wording. Put the citation close to the borrowed material so the reader can see which ideas belong to which source. A paragraph that switches between sources may need more than one citation.

Narrative citation List and Alexander (2019) propose a framework for using multiple texts. Name the authors in the sentence when their position or role matters.

Parenthetical citation Reading across documents involves several phases (List & Alexander, 2019). Use parentheses when the idea is the focus of the sentence.

One author
Hyland (2005) in the sentence, or (Hyland, 2005) in parentheses.
Two authors
Use both surnames each time. Write “and” in the sentence and “&” in parentheses.
Three or more authors
Usually use the first surname and “et al.” from the first citation, as in Bråten et al. (2009) or (Bråten et al., 2009). Use the full author information in the reference entry according to APA rules.

A paraphrase normally needs the author and year. A page number can help the reader locate a specific idea in a long work. For a direct quotation, add a page number or another locator such as a paragraph number. Enclose a quotation of fewer than 40 words in quotation marks. Use block formatting for quotations of 40 words or more.

APA author–date guidanceAPA quotation guidance

Build the reference for the source type

Start with the author, date, title and source. Locate these details on the publication itself or its publisher record. The format changes according to the type of work. Compare the title formatting and source details in the following entries.

Journal article

Italicise the journal name and volume. Give the issue and page range or article number when available. Present the DOI as a link.

Book

American Psychological Association. (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association (7th ed.). https://doi.org/10.1037/0000165-000

Italicise the book title and put the edition after it. Include the publisher when it differs from the author. Here the organisation is both author and publisher.

Webpage

American Psychological Association. (2022, July). Paraphrases. APA Style. https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/paraphrasing

Use the specific page title and link. Use the date shown for that content, or “n.d.” when the page has no publication or update date. Omit the site name when it is the same as the author.

Alphabetise the reference list by first author or organisation, preserve the author order within each entry and use a hanging indent. Cross-check in both directions. A cited article should be easy to find in the list, and a listed work should have a purpose in the text.

4.2 · Process of academic writing

Plan, draft and revise.

Planning, composing and reviewing recur as you write. A new source can change your thesis. An early draft can reveal a gap that sends you back to reading. This recursive view follows Flower and Hayes (1981) and Hayes (2012). The kind of text you are producing helps you decide what to plan and review (Badger & White, 2000).

4.2.1 Pre-writing

Background reading

Underline the command word, topic and scope before looking for sources. “Evaluate whether peer rehearsal should be required before microteaching” asks for a reasoned judgement about a requirement. A useful plan needs criteria, such as preparation and observed teaching performance, plus evidence that speaks to each criterion.

Give your question a purpose. In the example, the answer matters because a compulsory activity takes time away from other forms of preparation. That consequence helps explain why the quality of the evidence matters. Booth et al. (2016) show how writers can move from a topic to a question and explain why the answer is worth knowing.

  1. Turn the broad topic into a question. Ask what the reader needs to decide.
  2. Read with that question in mind. Record each source’s main claim, useful evidence, method or context, and one limitation. Save its publication details and any relevant page number.
  3. Group evidence by issue. Note which sources support, qualify or challenge your initial position.
  4. Draft a provisional thesis. Adjust its scope when the available evidence is narrower than the question.

Broad topic Peer rehearsal in teacher education.

Focused question What do the practice sources support about requiring rehearsal before microteaching?

Provisional thesis Teacher educators could offer peer rehearsal as a preparation option while collecting stronger evidence on classroom transitions before making it compulsory.

Evidence plan Use Source A for perceived readiness and Source B for observed transitions. Explain why these are different measures.

Considering the audience

A teacher educator deciding on course design needs the evidence and its limits. A fellow trainee preparing for tomorrow’s session needs a concrete procedure and a short example. Both readers can use the same topic, while the content, language and level of explanation change with the purpose. Genre and academic literacies research explain why expectations also vary by task and discipline (Hyland, 2007; Johns, 2008; Lea & Street, 1998; Martin & Rose, 2008; Wingate, 2012).

Swales (1990, 2004) uses the Create a Research Space (CARS) model to describe how research introductions establish a topic, locate a question or gap, and explain the present contribution. Here is how those moves can be adapted for an essay evaluating rehearsal.

4.2.2 While writing

Paragraphing

Give each paragraph a controlling point that advances the thesis. Use the topic sentence to orient the reader, then develop the point through evidence, analysis, examples or comparison. Check that every sentence contributes to the paragraph’s purpose and that the next paragraph follows logically.

PEEL is a planning scaffold. Check for a point, relevant evidence, an explanation of what the evidence means and a link to the wider argument where helpful. Toulmin’s model gives the explanation a sharper focus. The claim is the judgement, the grounds are its support, and the warrant is the reasoning that makes the support relevant. Qualification and rebuttal help the writer account for the conditions and limitations of the argument (Toulmin, 1958/2003).

Paraphrasing ideas

Paraphrasing is a way of using a source for a purpose in your own argument. Read the passage, decide which idea your paragraph needs, set the original aside, write the idea in your own sentence structure, then check the meaning, scope and wording against the source. Add a citation even when you have reworded the idea. Research on second-language source use helps explain why this process needs practice (Hirvela & Du, 2013; Keck, 2014; Pecorari, 2003; Wette, 2010).

Simulated source “After an optional peer rehearsal, nine of twelve volunteers reported feeling more confident about giving classroom directions.”

Too close “Following an optional peer practice, nine of twelve volunteers reported feeling more confident about giving classroom instructions.” Most of the sentence’s structure and several phrases remain the same.

Effective paraphrase “In Source A, three quarters of the volunteers who rehearsed said they felt more confident about giving directions (9 of 12 respondents).” The wording and structure change while the report, group and denominator remain clear.

Supporting details

Choose a detail that does a specific job. A definition can clarify the criterion. A finding can support a claim. A comparison can show why two results lead to different conclusions. After each detail, explain what it supports and how the method limits the inference.

Graff and Birkenstein (2024) frame source-based writing as a conversation. Represent a source’s position fairly, then state how your argument agrees, qualifies, extends or challenges it. In the rehearsal example, the survey supports a statement about reported confidence. The observation table addresses behaviour. An evidence-based response can acknowledge the survey result while qualifying the case for making rehearsal compulsory.

Study a paragraph with two academic sources

When trainees compare academic texts, they should record why each source deserves weight in their argument. Bråten et al. (2009) examined the relationship between judging source trustworthiness and constructing meaning across texts. List and Alexander (2019) place the use of multiple texts within a process that extends from preparation to the production of a response. In a class synthesis task, students could therefore record both the finding they intend to use and the reason they regard its source as appropriate. This makes the judgement available for review when they draft the paragraph.

Read the paragraph in three parts. The first sentence makes a recommendation. The next two sentences give the contributions of two sources and attribute them separately. The last two sentences develop a classroom implication. That implication is the writer’s application of the readings.

Citations and referencing while drafting

Add the citation as soon as you use a source. Keep a running reference list with author, year, title, publication details and DOI or URL where applicable. If one paragraph uses several sources, place each citation where its contribution is clear. Before moving on, read the paragraph and mark which sentences present evidence and which explain your own reasoning.

4.2.3 Post-writing

Editing and proofreading

Begin with a revision of the argument. Write a short phrase beside each paragraph describing what it does. This reverse outline helps you identify repetition, missing evidence and ideas in the wrong order. After the reasoning is sound, edit the language and proofread for accuracy.

Initial draft “Rehearsal prove that trainees are better teachers. The study had six good sessions, so all trainees should rehearse.”

Revise the judgement Narrow the argument to one observed behaviour and add the comparison group. Source B records a one-session difference in clear transitions. The groups had no comparable baseline, so the result gives limited support for a requirement.

Edit for clarity “Six of twelve rehearsed sessions met the transition criterion, compared with five of twelve unrehearsed sessions.” This identifies the groups, outcome and denominator without the vague phrase “good sessions”.

Proofread Check agreement, punctuation, spelling, number consistency and the placement of the attribution. Compare every in-text citation with the reference list and check the reference details against the source.

Check the conclusion against the introduction

Return to the question the introduction set up. State the judgement that follows from the body of the essay, bring the main evidence together and identify any limitation that affects the decision.

Worked conclusion “The simulated evidence supports offering peer rehearsal as a preparation option. The confidence survey describes how volunteers felt, while the observation table shows a small difference in one aspect of teaching. A more comparable observation design would be needed before recommending rehearsal as a programme requirement.”

Use feedback to decide what to change and why. If a peer says “add evidence”, identify the particular claim that lacks support and decide whether it needs a stronger source, more explanation or a narrower scope. Carless and Boud (2018) connect feedback literacy with making judgements and taking action on feedback.
