Topic 3

Academic Reading

CLO2. Analyse critically a variety of academic texts (C4, PLO2, KKG2).

A trainee preparing a microteaching session might consult a course reading, a classroom observation table and a peer’s account of the lesson. Each text gives access to different information. Academic reading involves deciding what each source contributes, how its evidence was produced and what can reasonably be concluded when the sources are read together.

3.1 Reading across multiple texts

Linear texts

Read the title, opening and headings to establish purpose. Then follow the argument through topic sentences, connections and examples. When an author uses a term such as participation, find out what the term refers to in that text. Speaking once, submitting work and attending a lesson measure different things.

Non-linear texts

Before interpreting a chart, table or diagram, identify its title, groups, units, denominator and time frame. Use a specific cell or feature as evidence and compare like with like. When prose refers to a figure, check whether the figure measures the same outcome as the prose describes.

Worked example. A simulated survey says 9 of 12 trainees who rehearsed reported greater confidence. An observation table records clear transitions in 6 of 12 rehearsed sessions and 5 of 12 unrehearsed sessions. The survey concerns how trainees felt. The table concerns one observed teaching behaviour. Both can inform a decision about rehearsal, while the evidence for each outcome remains separate.

3.2 Text types

Cause and effect
Identify the proposed cause, outcome and mechanism. Check whether the evidence rules out other explanations or simply shows that events occurred together.
Compare and contrast
Identify the comparison criteria and use them consistently. Comparing confidence in one group with classroom transitions in another mixes different measures.
Problem–solution
Check how the author establishes the problem, how the proposal addresses it and what the proposal would require in practice.
Expository
Trace how a text explains, defines or classifies a subject. Separate the central explanation from examples and examine whether any important step is missing.
Argumentative
Locate the claim, reasons, evidence and assumptions linking them. Assess whether the author addresses a serious alternative interpretation.

Academic texts can combine structures. A report may use exposition to explain the procedure, comparison to present results and argument to recommend a change. Identify the structure operating at the point you are reading.

3.3 Reading and viewing skills

Differentiate facts and opinions
A factual assertion is open to verification. An opinion expresses a judgement or preference. An interpretation links evidence to a possible meaning. “Six of twelve sessions met the criterion” is checkable. “The rehearsal was worthwhile” requires criteria and reasons.
Recognise bias and objectivity
Check what the author selected, omitted and foregrounded. A survey of volunteers may overrepresent trainees who favour rehearsal. A balanced tone can coexist with a selective sample.
Evaluate arguments
Test relevance, sufficiency and credibility. Ask whether the cited evidence supports the claim as stated, particularly when the author moves from association to causation or from one class to a whole programme.
Identify tone, purpose and point of view
Use wording and framing as evidence. “Clearly transforms teaching” signals a more certain tone than “may support preparation”. Purpose concerns what the writer wants to achieve. Point of view concerns whose position or experience shapes the account.
Process texts strategically
Survey the text to orient yourself, skim for the central message, scan for a specific value or term, then read the relevant passage closely. Annotate a claim, its evidence and one question. Record the page, figure or section while it is in view.
Link ideas across texts
Record which source supports, qualifies, extends or challenges an idea and on what basis. A synthesis matrix can align rows by issue or outcome while keeping authors, methods and limits visible.
Summarise texts
Condense a source around its main contribution and preserve qualifications that change the meaning. “Most surveyed volunteers reported greater confidence” retains scope that “rehearsal increased confidence” loses.
Synthesise information
Organise several sources around a common question. State their relationship, then explain what the combined evidence supports. Attribution allows the reader to trace which source supports each part of the synthesis.

Worked synthesis. The survey suggests that some volunteers perceived a benefit from rehearsal, while the observation table shows a small descriptive difference in clear transitions. Together, they give a reason to investigate rehearsal further. Establishing whether rehearsal improves classroom transitions would require a more comparable design and a clear observation criterion.

Apply it. Read two texts about one teaching practice. Record what each measured, one result and one limitation. Write a synthesis paragraph organised around the shared question. Underline the phrase that explains how the sources relate.

Topic 4

Academic Writing

CLO3. Compose writing for a variety of academic purposes and contexts (C6, A4, PLO2, PLO11, KKG5, KEP10).

In academic writing, the reader should be able to identify the writer’s judgement, trace the evidence and see how the two connect. The work begins before drafting, when the writer decides what the task requires and reads the sources with that purpose in mind. It continues through revision, when a claim can be narrowed or reorganised in response to the evidence.

4.1 Characteristics of academic writing

Formal tone
Choose a measured register. Replace conversational intensifiers or broad judgements with a specific, supportable claim. For example, “Rehearsal worked really well” can become “Nine of twelve volunteers reported feeling more confident after rehearsing.”
Third-person perspective
Foreground the topic, evidence or participants in a conventional academic essay. For example, “You can see that the groups are different” can become “The table records a difference of one session.” First person may be appropriate in reflective writing or when a writer needs to identify their own research actions. Follow the assignment and disciplinary convention.
Diction
Use the word that accurately describes the evidence. Reported identifies a self-report. Observed indicates that a behaviour was recorded. Suggests suits a tentative interpretation. Reserve stronger causal language for a design that can support it.
Academic conventions
Distinguish your voice from your sources. Cite ideas, data and wording drawn from others. In APA 7, use the author and year for a paraphrase. Use quotation marks for a short direct quotation and supply a locator, such as a page or paragraph number. Match the in-text citation to the full reference for works that belong in the reference list.

APA 7 worked example. Narrative: List and Alexander (2019) propose a framework for using multiple texts. Parenthetical: Multiple-text use can be understood as a process with distinct phases (List & Alexander, 2019). The first form names the authors in the sentence. The second places the attribution after the idea.

4.2 Process of academic writing

4.2.1 Pre-writing

Read the task verb, purpose and audience. An essay that evaluates a teaching practice requires criteria and evidence. A reflection requires attention to what the trainee learned from experience. Carry out background reading, record the source details and note the evidence in your own words. Use the readings to narrow the question and formulate a provisional claim that the evidence can support.

4.2.2 While writing

Build a paragraph around a claim that advances the argument. Supply relevant supporting detail, attribute it, then explain how it supports or qualifies the claim. A citation establishes where a detail came from. The reasoning is still the writer’s responsibility.

To paraphrase, read for meaning, set the passage aside, restate the relevant idea in a structure that serves your own paragraph, then check for accuracy and excessive overlap. Preserve the original scope and qualification. Record citations and references as you draft, especially when a paragraph draws on more than one source.

Worked paragraph. Rehearsal may help trainees feel prepared, although evidence of clearer classroom transitions remains limited. In the simulated survey, nine of twelve volunteers reported greater confidence. In the observation table, six of twelve rehearsed sessions met the transition criterion, compared with five of twelve unrehearsed sessions. The first measure captures self-reported confidence and the second records behaviour. A lecturer could offer rehearsal as a preparation activity and collect comparable observation data before making it a programme requirement.

4.2.3 Post-writing

Revise the claim, order and support first. Reverse-outline the draft by writing each paragraph’s function in the margin. Then edit sentences for coherence, tone, diction and clarity. Proofread grammar, spelling, punctuation, formatting and citation–reference matches in the final pass. Return to background reading when the argument needs a better source.

Apply it. Plan a paragraph for a teacher educator, draft it using two sources and revise one overstated sentence. Record the evidence that justified the revision. In a real assignment, replace the simulated source labels with traceable APA 7 citations and references.
